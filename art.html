Our birds: the Daybird art

A silhouette plus one accent. Every bird is a flat ink shape with a single amber mark on the one field mark a beginner uses to name it.

The rule

Start from the Daybird mark: a tufted titmouse in ink, the branch in amber. Each species gets the same treatment. Draw the whole bird as one ink silhouette, facing left and perched where that is how you meet it, with the proportions a birder checks first: how big the head is, how long the tail is, what shape the bill is. Then add exactly one amber mark, and put it on the field mark a new birder should look for, so the art teaches the same thing the brief does. Nothing else gets color. No outlines, no gradients, no second accent. If a bird needs two colors to read, the shape is not done yet.

The mark

A tufted titmouse: crest, stubby bill, long tail, perched. The branch is its accent. The mark keeps its amber branch; a species never gets one, because the amber is spoken for.

Sizes

Checked at 24, 48 and 120 px. At 24 px only the shape has to read: which bird, which posture. The amber reads from 48 px up, and at 24 px the smallest marks (the downy's nape spot, the cardinal's bill, the goldfinch's wing bar) drop out, so use 48 px or larger anywhere the accent matters. If a mark still vanishes at 48 px, make the mark bigger, not the bird.

Do and don't

Do

  • One accent only, on the field mark a beginner uses.
  • Silhouette faces left. A flying bird is seen from below, head up.
  • Perch, trunk, ground or water line is optional and always ink.
  • Keep the eye as a small cut-out and one thin wing crease; they are what make a flat shape read as a bird.
  • Check proportions against a field guide: crest, bill, tail, legs, posture.

Don't

  • No outlines, strokes around the body, or gradients.
  • No second color, even when the bird is famous for one (the cardinal stays ink; only its bill is amber).
  • No amber branch under a species; that belongs to the mark.
  • No text inside a species file. Labels live in the callout versions.
  • No guessing a shape you have not checked. Ask a birder.
