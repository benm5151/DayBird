Why the bird doesn't match the book

The guide paints one tidy adult per species. Real birds come in two sexes, several ages, and a fresh and a worn version of each. Six things that explain most of the mismatches.

White-throated Sparrow

1 of 6

One mark for the species, or one odd bird?

Field guides paint one bird per species, so when yours has a tan eyebrow instead of a white one it feels like a different bird, or a mistake. Usually it is neither. White-throated Sparrows come in two forms, tan-striped and white-striped, and both are normal adults; they even prefer to pair with the opposite form. The marks that define a species are the ones every individual carries: here, the white throat and the yellow spot in front of the eye. Crown color, how bright the yellow is, how heavy the streaking, those vary from bird to bird. Learn which marks belong to the species and which to the individual, and the odd bird stops being a puzzle.

If the defining marks are there, it is that species, however scruffy the rest looks.

American Robin

2 of 6

Young birds look different

A robin with a spotted breast is still a robin. For its first summer a young bird wears juvenile plumage: spots and streaks that help it hide, a shorter tail, and often a pale, swollen-looking corner to the mouth. By fall most have molted into something close to the adult, but some, like young hawks and gulls, take years. The guide shows you the finished adult; from June through September half the birds in your yard are not finished. When something looks almost like a robin, a cardinal or a bluebird but softer, spottier and clumsier, think young bird before you think rare bird.

From June to September, a bird that looks almost right is probably a young one.

Northern Cardinal

3 of 6

Male and female

The male cardinal is on the cover; the female is the brown bird beside him, with the same crest, the same red bill and a wash of red on the wings and tail. In many songbirds the sexes look different, and the guide's big picture is usually the male. The female tends to be duller for a good reason: she sits on the nest. Shape is the way through. A female cardinal has the cardinal's crest and heavy bill, a female Red-winged Blackbird has the blackbird's pointed bill and posture, and a female goldfinch has the goldfinch's stubby bill and bouncing flight. Match the shape first, then look for a faded version of the male's colors.

Shape is the same in both sexes; color is not. Match the shape first.

European Starling

4 of 6

Fresh, worn, and in between

A starling in October is covered in white spots and has a dark bill. The same bird in April is glossy purple-green with a yellow bill and no spots. Nothing was replaced in between; the pale tips of the fresh fall feathers simply wore away over the winter. Feathers wear and fade all year, then are replaced in a molt, usually once after nesting and, for some birds, partly again in spring. A molting bird can look patchy, with gaps in the wings or a half-and-half head, and a worn bird in July can look faded and ragged. Neither is sick, and neither is a different species.

A bird can look like two different birds in one year and never change species.

5 of 6

The wing, in three parts

Guides say primaries, secondaries and coverts as if everyone knew. On a spread wing, the primaries are the long outer feathers at the wingtip, the ones that look like fingers on a soaring hawk. The secondaries are the shorter inner flight feathers along the trailing edge, closer to the body. The coverts are the rows of small feathers covering the base of all of those; on a folded wing they are the shoulder and the bars across it. Wingbars are pale tips on the coverts. So "two white wingbars" means two pale lines across the folded wing, and "pale primary edges" means thin light lines along the wingtip.

Primaries at the tip, secondaries toward the body, coverts over the base of both.

Blue Jay

6 of 6

Acting odd is normal

A young bird on the lawn that will not fly away, fluffy, short-tailed, calling and calling, is almost always fine. Most songbirds leave the nest a few days before they can fly well and spend that time on the ground or in low branches while the parents feed them. The parents are nearby even when you cannot see them. The rule wildlife rehabilitators give: if it has feathers and is hopping, leave it, or lift it into a shrub if a cat is near; if it is naked and pink, put it back in the nest. Loud begging, strange posture, sitting still as you walk up: all normal for a fledgling in June and July.

A feathered youngster on the ground is a normal part of summer, not an emergency.
